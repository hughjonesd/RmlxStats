# Review of the RmlxStats fuzz-testing strategy

## Bottom line

The right backbone for a package like **RmlxStats** is **differential fuzzing against trusted reference implementations**, plus a smaller number of **metamorphic/property tests** and a still smaller number of **simulation-based statistical validation tests**. That is also broadly where current best practice points: the scientific-software literature recommends metamorphic testing because exact output oracles are often unavailable, while mature libraries outside R add reusable invariant suites and API-level checks on top of ordinary examples. In practice, that means your `lm`/`glm`/`glmnet` fuzz tests are probably doing something valuable already, but they can only justify a narrow claim: *for the generated problems you tried, under the comparison metric and tolerance you chose, the implementation stayed close to the reference*. They do **not**, by themselves, establish unbiasedness, standard-error correctness, confidence-interval coverage, or bootstrap validity. citeturn42search0turn63view5turn42search1turn46search0

There is one package-specific constraint that matters throughout: the public docs say entity["organization","RmlxStats","r package"] targets large-\(N\), large-\(P\) use on GPU hardware and that its calculations use **float32** precision, not float64. That means tolerances copied from ordinary double-precision R tests are not automatically meaningful; they have to be interpreted relative to float32 arithmetic, the conditioning of the problem, and whether you are checking coefficients, fitted values, objectives, or inferential summaries. citeturn24search0turn29search3turn59calculator0

## What tolerances really tell you

A tolerance in `testthat::expect_equal()` is not a simple “maximum absolute error” unless you force it to be. `testthat` delegates floating-point comparisons to an `all.equal()`-style algorithm, and both `testthat` and base R document that this is usually **relative** tolerance, switching to something effectively absolute only when the target is near zero. Likewise, entity["organization","NumPy","python array library"]’s `assert_allclose()` uses the explicit rule `atol + rtol * abs(desired)`. So a bare tolerance number is only interpretable together with the comparison scale. citeturn51search0turn50search8turn44search1

For your package, that implies three practical rules. First, a single absolute tolerance on raw coefficients is usually poor evidence, because coefficient vectors can move a lot under collinearity even when predictions barely change. Second, tolerances are much more meaningful on **standardised quantities**: standardised coefficients, linear predictors, fitted probabilities, residual norms, deviance, objective values, and KKT residuals. Third, the tolerance budget should widen as the design matrix becomes more ill-conditioned. Condition number is the right summary statistic here: by definition it measures how sensitive the linear-algebra problem is to perturbation, and numerical-stability texts treat conditioning as central to interpreting forward error. citeturn62view6turn42search2

So what does a tolerance “say” about reliability? If it is attached to **predictions or objective values on standardised data**, it says something real about numerical agreement with the reference routine. If it is attached to **raw coefficients on unscaled, correlated designs**, it often says very little. In fact, in correlated designs the best implementations can legitimately differ in coefficients while still agreeing closely on fitted values, loss, and selected support. That is why your discovery of a bug that only appeared with correlation is fully consistent with the literature: correlated and nearly singular inputs are exactly where coefficient-based oracles become weakest and metamorphic or invariant checks become most useful. citeturn62view6turn42search2turn63view5

For inferential tests, the same point becomes even sharper. Coverage or bootstrap tests need tolerances that account for **Monte Carlo error in the test itself**. A nominal 95% coverage rate estimated from 1,000 replications has a 95% Monte Carlo half-width of about **1.35 percentage points**; with 10,000 replications it is still about **0.43 points**. So a test that “expects 95% coverage” without an acceptance band is statistically ill-posed. The older statistical literature on simulation testing of variance estimators and bootstrap coverage makes exactly this point: the simulation itself introduces uncertainty, and that uncertainty must be part of the oracle. citeturn63view4turn43search1turn58calculator0turn58calculator1

## The most important gaps to look for

The obvious danger for a fuzz suite in statistical computing is not that it is too random, but that it is **random only in easy ways**. Mature practice is to mix random generation with deliberately structured stress cases. Outside R, entity["organization","scikit-learn","machine learning library"] formalises this by shipping reusable estimator checks for shapes, inputs, API semantics, and behavioural invariants. Scientific-software testing research goes one step further and recommends metamorphic relations precisely because examples alone tend to miss the structurally hard cases. citeturn42search1turn42search0turn63view5

For your current scope, the most obvious missing scenarios would be:

Correlated predictors and **near-rank-deficient** designs, because these are the natural adversarial cases for `lm`, `glm`, and lasso/elastic-net paths. If a test suite mostly samples independent Gaussian columns, it will overestimate robustness.

Predictor **rescaling and recentering** transformations. A good metamorphic test is not “does coefficient 7 stay the same?” but “after rescaling \(x_j\), do the fitted values, deviance, and appropriately rescaled coefficient remain consistent?” That kind of relation is much closer to the mathematical contract.

For `glm`, cases near **complete or quasi-separation**, rare outcomes, extreme offsets, extreme weights, and very unbalanced probabilities. Those are where IRLS-style routines and line searches get numerically awkward.

For `glmnet`, cases where support is unstable, where multiple predictors are highly correlated, and where the chosen \(\lambda\) is near a support transition. The `glmnet` docs and papers explicitly note pathwise fitting, warm starts, and screening/strong rules; the strong-rules paper also discusses violations, which is exactly why KKT checks matter. citeturn52search3turn52search5turn52search1

For `cv.glmnet`, exact equality is especially brittle unless you lock down **`foldid` and the lambda grid**. The official docs state that by default the full model gets a master lambda sequence and each fold gets its own sequence, so fuzz tests that compare raw selected `lambda.min` or `lambda.1se` values without fixing those ingredients can fail or pass for the wrong reasons. citeturn45search0turn62view2

For future `pca`, you should not compare raw eigenvectors elementwise except in contrived cases. The official docs for `PCA` and `randomized_svd` emphasise solver changes by size/shape and note that randomized methods require oversampling and power-iteration choices for slow spectral decay and noisy problems. The right oracle is **subspace agreement**, explained variance, singular values, reconstruction error, and invariance to column sign flips, not exact vector orientation. citeturn48search0turn61search0

## Large N and large P

This is where your current emphasis is absolutely right, but also where the small-to-large extrapolation is most dangerous.

You can usually generalise from small tests to large tests **only if** the same code path, solver, numeric type, and stopping rules are being exercised. Once size changes the algorithm, all bets are off. The clearest documented example is PCA: the solver can switch between exact and randomized methods depending on shape, and the randomized method has its own tuning knobs for conditioning and slow spectral decay. So a suite that proves correctness on small exact-SVD cases has not yet probed the large-matrix failure modes. citeturn48search0turn61search0

Something similar can happen in regularised regression. `glmnet` is pathwise, uses warm starts, exploits sparsity, and cross-validation can use fold-specific lambda sequences. In that regime, large-\(P\) tests should explicitly watch the things that small toy tests miss: active-set stability, KKT residuals, objective monotonicity along the path, support changes under near-duplicate columns, and reproducibility under fixed folds. If an implementation uses screening ideas akin to strong rules, then large-\(P\) tests are precisely where mistaken discards are most plausible. citeturn52search5turn52search1turn45search0

The best balance between speed and completeness is therefore **tiered**. Small tests should remain oracle-rich and exacting. Medium tests should be property-heavy. Large tests should be fewer, but deliberately pathological and representative of the regimes your users actually care about: \(N \gg P\), \(P \gg N\), high correlation blocks, duplicate or nearly duplicate columns, broad dynamic range, and near-separation. Because the package is explicitly positioned for large \(N\)/\(P\) GPU use, I would give those structured stress cases more priority than yet more easy random draws. citeturn24search0turn48search0turn61search0

## Specific recommendations for the mlxs suite

For **`mlxs_lm`**, make the primary oracle **prediction-space** and **objective-space** agreement, not just coefficient agreement. On each fuzz draw, record condition number, compare standardised coefficients only after centring/scaling, and add metamorphic checks for row permutation, column permutation, predictor rescaling, and duplicated/aliased columns. Keep one deliberate family of highly correlated block designs in every run; do not leave that to chance. citeturn62view6turn42search2turn63view5

For **`mlxs_glm`**, separate numerical tests from inferential tests. Numerical fuzzing should compare linear predictors, fitted probabilities, deviance, score/gradient norms, and convergence behaviour under offsets, weights, class imbalance, and near-separation. Inferential properties such as standard-error accuracy or CI coverage need a separate Monte Carlo harness with explicit acceptance bands. A single-run comparison to `glm()` cannot answer those questions. citeturn46search0turn63view4turn58calculator0

For **`mlxs_glmnet`**, compare more than coefficients: compare path monotonicity, objective values, support size, KKT residuals, and prediction error at a fixed user-supplied lambda grid. Include correlated groups, duplicated columns, and both \(N \gg P\) and \(P \gg N\) settings. If there is any screening or active-set pruning, add an explicit large-\(P\) check that the final active set satisfies KKT conditions for all discarded variables. citeturn52search5turn52search1

For **`mlxs_cv_glmnet`**, always fix `foldid` in tests that compare across implementations, and preferably also pass a common lambda sequence. Then compare the full CV curve, `cvm`/`cvsd`, and prediction error at the selected lambda, rather than demanding exact equality of `lambda.min` when the foldwise sequences may differ. Also add stability checks under repeated random folds, because reliability here is partly about *selection stability*, not only point agreement. citeturn45search0turn45search1

For future **`mlxs_pca`**, test subspace recovery, explained variance ratios, reconstruction error, and invariance to sign flips of components. Add explicit hard cases with slowly decaying singular spectra, nearly tied singular values, and large \(N\)/\(P\) matrices that trigger randomized solvers. Raw vector-by-vector equality is the wrong target. citeturn48search0turn61search0

For **`mlxs_boot`**, the key tests are not just equality to a reference resampling loop but **coverage**, variance estimation, and reproducibility under fixed resample indices. Use a small number of analytically simple problems where the truth is known, then treat the estimated coverage as a binomial quantity with Monte Carlo uncertainty. That is the only way the tolerance around “95% coverage” becomes meaningful. citeturn63view4turn43search1turn58calculator0turn58calculator1

At suite level, the best upgrade is to make your fuzz tests report **why** a case is hard: sample size, predictor count, condition number, family, correlation structure, sparsity, class balance, and whether the comparison was coefficient-space or prediction-space. That turns tolerances from magic constants into interpretable evidence.
